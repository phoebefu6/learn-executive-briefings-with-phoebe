Pick the format

The same decision can reach an executive as a one-page memo, a pre-read for a meeting, a six-page narrative read in silence, or four lines in an email. Each shape asks the reader for a different amount of time and tests the reasoning to a different depth. Tonight we choose on purpose: four formats, four questions that pick between them, and what two famous sources on long memos and short ones actually say.

Part 1 · covers four executive formats

Four shapes for one decision

5 min live

An executive format is a promise about the reader's time. A one-page decision memo promises a yes or no in a few minutes. A pre-read promises that the meeting can spend its time deciding instead of catching up. A narrative long memo asks for much more time and gives back reasoning that has been tested. A short bottom-line-first note asks for almost nothing because the reader already knows the story. Pick the promise first; the length follows.

LiveThe four executive formats, side by side3 min▶

Decision memo. One page, the decision on top, for a yes or no. It works when the reader can decide alone, or will decide in a meeting but only needs the case and the cost to do it. This is the default for most asks a data lead makes.

Pre-read. Any document sent before a meeting so the meeting decides instead of reads. The format is often a decision memo; what makes it a pre-read is the slot: it arrives early enough to be read, and the meeting starts from it. When to send it is session 6.

Narrative long memo. Several pages of full sentences, in the style Amazon describes, read in silence at the start of the meeting. It wins when the decision needs the reasoning tested, because prose forces the writer to connect each claim to the next, and the room can see where the connection breaks.

Bottom-line-first note. An email-length ask: the request, the date, one reason, and who else has seen it. It wins when the decision is small and the reader already has the context, so anything longer spends their time on what they know.

Self-studyWhy the format follows the meeting, not the topic3 min▶

A common mistake is to pick the format by how important the topic feels: a big programme gets a long document because it is big. The tree asks something else. Is anyone deciding, and where? A €1.4 million proposal decided in one executive meeting needs the meeting to be ready, which is a pre-read; it does not automatically need six pages.

The reverse mistake is just as common: a one-line email for a decision the reader has never heard of. If Marguerite had to ask "what is this?" before she could answer, the note was the wrong shape. Question 2 in the tree exists to catch that: small, and with context, both have to be true.

Where the generic triage lives. Choosing between an update, a report and a memo for any audience is taught in AI Writing, Updates, reports and decision memos ↗. The difference here is the executive end of that range: four formats an executive will see, and the conditions under which each one wins.

Part 2 · covers the narrative memo and the silent read

When the long memo wins

5 min live

The most quoted example of a long memo culture is Amazon's, and the most quoted version of it is wrong in a small but useful way. The founder's own account is narrower than "Amazon banned PowerPoint", and it describes a real cost: writing time, and meeting time spent reading. That cost is worth paying for some decisions and not for others.

LiveWhat the Bezos letter actually says3 min▶

In his 2017 letter to shareholders, published in April 2018 as an SEC exhibit (read at source), Jeff Bezos says Amazon does not do slide presentations in its meetings. Instead it writes "narratively structured six-page memos". The memos are read silently at the start of each meeting, which he compares to a study hall, and he says a great memo probably takes a week or more to write.

That is narrower than the popular line. The letter describes what Amazon does in its own meetings; it does not say slides are banned everywhere, and it is not a study comparing the two. The often-told story of a 2004 email banning slides in senior-team meetings is reported in secondary accounts and was not read for these pages. Teach it as one company's practice, described by its founder.

Self-studyThe cost side, and when it is not worth it3 min▶

A narrative memo costs the writer days and costs every person in the meeting their reading time, before anyone speaks. That is worth it when the decision turns on the reasoning: a new strategy, a reorganisation, a bet where the room needs to see whether the argument holds. The silent read guarantees the memo is actually read, which is the problem a pre-read sent the night before often has.

It is not worth it when the reader needs a yes or no in four minutes and the reasoning is not in dispute. Sunniva's proposal sits in between: Halvard will test the numbers, but nobody at Fernhollow is arguing about whether wrong addresses cost money. Six pages would spend the meeting on reasoning nobody doubts. That is why her answer to question 4 is "partly", and why the next Part matters.

Real world

The Bezos letter, at source. Bezos, J. (2018, April). 2017 Letter to Shareholders, SEC Form 8-K exhibit 99.1 (read at source). It names the six-page narrative memo, the silent read at the start of the meeting, and the week it takes to write a good one. It is a description of one company's habit by the person who set it, which makes it a strong example and a weak proof.

Part 3 · covers brevity and the bottom line

Brevity is a format decision

5 min live

Short is not the same as brief. A short memo can still bury its point under padding; a brief one puts each point in its own short paragraph and moves the detail somewhere the reader can choose to go. That move, from the body to an appendix, is a format decision, and it is the one Sunniva needs for Halvard.

LiveChurchill's Brevity minute3 min▶

On 9 August 1940 Winston Churchill circulated a memorandum to the War Cabinet titled "Brevity". The National Archives holds it as CAB 67/8/11 (catalogue read; the content below is reported from a published transcription, not read from the archive image). He complained that official papers were far too long, and asked for main points set out in "a series of short, crisp paragraphs".

The rest of the advice, as reported, is a format decision more than a style one: put complicated factors or statistics in an appendix; sometimes send only headings, to be expanded in conversation; and drop the padding phrases that fill a sentence without saying anything. Every one of those moves words somewhere, rather than deleting what the reader may need.

Self-studyBottom line up front, in one line2 min▶

Bottom line up front (BLUF) is the conclusion first, the support after. The US Army's correspondence regulation AR 25-50 is widely reported to require the recommendation or reason for writing early in the document (reported; the regulation itself could not be fetched). A 2010 Fort Jackson regulation, FJ REG 1-1, tells staff to place "the bottom line up front" and to use it alongside AR 25-50 (read at source).

BLUF and the pyramid are taught properly in Communication, Structure ↗. Here they matter for one reason: the bottom-line-first note in the tree is BLUF with nothing underneath, and the one-page memo is BLUF with an appendix underneath.

Real world

Churchill, Brevity, 1940. Churchill, W. S. (1940, 9 August). Brevity. War Cabinet memorandum, CAB 67/8/11, WP (G) (40) 211, The National Archives, Kew (catalogue read; text reported from a published transcription). A head of government writing in wartime asked for short paragraphs and an appendix for the numbers. The reason holds for any reader who decides a lot in a day: the body is for deciding, the appendix is for checking.

Split the body from the appendix. The body carries what the decider needs to say yes or no. The appendix carries what the checker needs to trust the numbers. For each item, ask: who needs this, and do they need it to decide or to check?

Name the slot it goes out in. A pre-read has to land before the meeting with time to be read. Exactly how far before, and what to do if it is not read, is session 6. For now, write down the meeting and who sees it first.

Check the choice against your reader card. Read the card from session 1 line by line. Does the format serve what the reader is measured on and what they fear? If a line pulls against the format, change the format, not the card.

Choose the format before the second draft. The first draft can be any shape that helps you think. The format decision tells you what the second draft cuts, what it moves to an appendix and what it promises the reader about their time.
