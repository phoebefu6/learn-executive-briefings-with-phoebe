Who reads it, and the decision on top

A memo to an executive is read between two other things, by someone who has to decide something and has a few minutes to find out what. Tonight we start with that reader: what they look at first, whether your memo asks them to decide or only to know, and why the title is the most valuable line on the page. By the end you have the top of one real memo written: a title that states the decision and a first sentence that carries it.

Part 1 · covers the reader and what they look at first

The reader has minutes, not an afternoon

5 min live

An executive does not read a memo the way its writer did. The writer went top to bottom, probably several times. The reader looks at the title, glances down the headings, reads the first line of a few paragraphs, and decides whether the rest needs them. Whatever they need to act on has to be in the places they look first.

LiveFour minutes is a design target, not a finding3 min▶

You will hear that executives spend some exact number of seconds on a memo, or read only the first two lines. No study behind either claim turned up, so neither is quoted here. What is measured is reading speed: a 2019 meta-analysis of 190 studies put adults' average silent reading of English non-fiction at 238 words a minute (Brysbaert, abstract read). At that pace four careful minutes is under a thousand words, before the reader has thought about anything.

So four minutes is a design target: write as if the reader has that long, at most, and will spend most of it deciding whether to spend more. If your memo works for that reader, it also works for the one who reads every word.

Self-studyWhat the reader is doing before and after3 min▶

Marguerite opens Sunniva's memo between a board call and a depot visit. Before it, she was thinking about something else entirely; after it, she will either forward it to Halvard with a question, put it on the agenda for 14 November, or file it. A memo that tells her in its first lines which of those three to do saves her the work of finding out.

That is the whole job of the top of the page: get the reader from "what is this?" to "what do I do?" before their attention moves on. Everything below the top is there to make that answer safe to give.

Real world

Readers follow the cues you give them. Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking work on web pages found that readers fall into an F-shaped scan, the top lines and then the left edge, when a page gives them no strong cues; the 2017 write-up calls that pattern "bad for users and businesses" and recommends informative headings and bold key phrases (read at source). It is web research, not a study of executives reading memos, so session 4 treats the skim order as a stated model rather than a fact.

Part 2 · covers decision memos and information memos

Is the reader deciding, or only knowing?

5 min live

Every memo to an executive is one of two kinds. A decision memo asks the reader to do something: approve, choose, fund, stop. An information memo changes what the reader knows and asks for nothing. Most bad memos are a decision memo written as an information memo: an update with an ask hidden somewhere in the middle.

LiveThe update with an ask inside it3 min▶

Sunniva's first draft was called "Customer data at Fernhollow Freight". It opened with how the company grew from four depots to eleven, walked through the measurements in the order her team made them, compared three options, and ended with "I recommend we build it, and I need your decision at the executive meeting on 14 November". Every sentence was true. It read like an update, so Marguerite would have read it like one.

The test in the figure settles it. After reading, Marguerite has to approve or refuse a €1.4 million spend by a date. That is a decision memo, and a decision memo puts the decision where the reader looks first. Finding what you are actually asking for is taught in Communication, The ask ↗; here we assume you have found it and need to place it.

Self-studyWhen an information memo is the right call3 min▶

Not every page needs an ask. A risk the executive should know about before someone else tells them, a result that changes a number they quote, a heads-up that a decision is coming next month: these are information memos, and inventing an ask for them wastes the reader's attention on a decision that is not ready.

The discipline is the same shape. An information memo puts its finding on top, in one line, in the title if it fits. "Q3 churn is up 2 points, driven by one region" beats "Q3 churn analysis". If next month's decision memo will follow, say so in the last line, so the reader knows the ask is coming.

Real world

The Army writes it into its rules. A US Army training-centre regulation from 2010 tells staff to write papers in a logical format "placing the bottom line up front", alongside the Army's correspondence regulation AR 25-50 (the training-centre regulation read at source; AR 25-50's own wording reported). Bottom line up front and the pyramid are taught in Communication, Structure ↗. The one-line recap: the conclusion first, the support after.

Part 3 · covers the title and the reader card

The title states the decision

5 min live

The title is the one line every reader reads. A topic title ("Customer data at Fernhollow Freight") tells them what the memo is about. A decision title ("Decision needed by 14 November: approve the shared customer record") tells them what to do and by when, before they open it. Write the title last and test it first.

LiveWriting the decision title3 min▶

A decision title has a verb the reader can do ("approve", "choose", "stop", "fund"), the object, and the date if there is one. It can be long: twelve words that tell Marguerite what to do beat four that make her open the memo to find out. If the decision is not yet sharp enough to fit in a title, the memo is not ready to send, and that is worth knowing before Marguerite finds out.

On the session 4 bench, the draft with the topic title does not reach the decision until 75.5 seconds into a modelled skim; the rewrite reaches the decision and the deadline at 1.7 seconds, from the title alone. The seconds come from a stated model, and the comparison is the point: one line moved the decision from the end of the read to the start.

LiveThe reader card: who you are writing to3 min▶

Before the title, write four lines about the reader: who decides, who they check with, what they are measured on, and what this proposal costs them. Sunniva already has these from her stakeholder map: Marguerite signs, asks Halvard about the numbers and Odile about the depots, is measured on growth, margin and board confidence, and fears another IT programme the board will ask about. That last line tells Sunniva what Marguerite will look for: the cost, the risk, and whether Halvard has seen it.

The map itself is built in Stakeholder Management, Who actually decides ↗ and the scorecard and cost ledger in What they are measured on, and what they fear ↗. The brief you keep about your own boss, from Managing up ↗, is a relationship document; the memo is a decision document. Use the first to write the second, and never send the first.

Real world

Headings that carry the information. The same Nielsen Norman Group article advises starting headings and subheadings with "the words carrying most information" (read at source). A decision title is that advice applied to the one heading every reader sees.

Build-along

Write the top of one real memo

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Pick one memo you need to write in the next month. If you have none, use Sunniva's. You will decide what kind of memo it is, write the reader card, then the title, then the first sentence, and test the result the way a skimming reader would.

Decide which kind of memo it is. Ask the test question: what will the reader do differently after reading it? Write the answer in one line.

★ Step 1 · the kind of memoReader: Marguerite Vance, CEO After reading she will: approve or refuse a two-year customer-data platform at the 14 November meeting. Kind: decision memo

Write the reader card. Four lines from your stakeholder map. If a line is blank, that is the first thing to find out, before you write anything else.

★ Step 2 · reader cardDecides: Marguerite (signs above the capital threshold) Checks with: Halvard (the numbers), Odile (the depots) Measured on: growth, margin, board confidence Fears: another IT programme the board asks about

Write the title. A verb the reader can do, the object, the date. Then read it alone: could the reader act on the title if they read nothing else?

★ Step 3 · the titleBefore: Customer data at Fernhollow Freight After: Decision needed by 14 November: approve the shared customer record

Write the first sentence of the memo. It carries the decision, the ask, the deadline and the cost. It will be long; that is fine for one sentence. The risk comes later, in its own short section, and session 5 is about writing it.

★ Step 4 · the first sentenceI recommend one shared customer record for sales, the depots and billing, at €1.4 million over two years, and I ask you to approve it at the executive meeting on 14 November.

Run the skim test by hand. Read only the title and the first sentence of each paragraph, as a stranger would. Write down what you learned. If the decision, the ask or the deadline is not on your list, it is not on the reader's either.

★ Step 5 · the skim testTitle and first sentences only. What did I learn? decision yes (title, first sentence) ask yes (first sentence) deadline yes (title) cost yes (first sentence) risk not yet: write it in session 5

Write the title last, test it first. Draft the memo in whatever order helps you think. Then rewrite the top for the reader, and check it by reading only what a skimmer reads. Session 4 turns that check into a computed bench you can paste your own text into.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Finish the top of your memo. All five steps for a real memo you owe someone. If the reader card has a blank line, book the conversation that fills it.
  2. Sort your last five sent documents. For each, was it a decision memo or an information memo, and did its title say so? Count how many titles named a topic instead of a decision.
  3. Find one buried ask. In something you received this week, find the sentence that asks you to do something. How far down was it?
  4. Bring the memo you most need to send. Session 2 decides which format it should take: a one-page decision memo, a pre-read, a long narrative or a short note.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Sunniva's draft is titled "Customer data at Fernhollow Freight" and ends by asking for a decision on 14 November. What is wrong with it?

After reading, Marguerite must approve or refuse a spend by a date, so it is a decision memo, and decision memos put the decision on top. A assumes a reader with time to spare; C contradicts the ask Sunniva actually has; D confuses length with content.

2 · Which claim about executive reading is safe to put in your own memo guidance?

Only D has a study under it: a meta-analysis of 190 studies. No source was found for A or B, and the goldfish figure is traced to nothing in Focus, What attention is ↗. Four minutes is a design target, not a finding.

3 · What belongs on the reader card, and what happens to it?

The card comes from the stakeholder map and tells you what the reader will look for: for Marguerite, the cost, the risk and whether Halvard has seen it. It is a planning tool, like the boss brief from Managing up, and never part of the memo.
