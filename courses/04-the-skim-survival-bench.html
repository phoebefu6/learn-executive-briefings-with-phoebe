Part 0

Where we start

The four memos on the bench are constructed. They are four versions of the same Fernhollow Freight proposal from Sunniva Holm to Marguerite Vance: her first draft in the order she worked it out, the rewrite with the decision on top, an over-long version with everything in it, and a version with clear headings but the ask buried in paragraph 4. Every fact in them is the same; only the order and the length change. That is what makes the comparison fair, and it is why no number on the bench is a measurement of any real executive.

Part 1 · covers the reading rate, the skim order and the five elements

How the skim reader works

6 min live

The bench has one measured input, one assumption and one simplification, and it labels each. Knowing which is which is the difference between learning from it and being fooled by it.

LiveThe rate, and the assumption on top of it3 min▶

Brysbaert's 2019 meta-analysis of 190 studies and 18,573 readers put the average silent reading rate of adults in English at 238 words a minute for non-fiction, with most adults between 175 and 300 (abstract read). The abstract also notes that this is lower than the figures often cited. That is the measured part.

A skim is faster than a read, and a skimming reader understands less. How much faster, for an executive reading a memo, is not something a study pinned down that this bench could find, so the bench calls it an assumption: a multiplier on 238, set to 1.5 by default (357 words a minute) and movable from 1.0 to 2.0. Run your conclusions at both ends of the slider before you trust them.

LiveThe reading order, and why it is a simplification3 min▶

The reader looks at the title, then every heading, then the first sentence of every paragraph, then bold phrases, then list items and table rows, and only then reads the remaining sentences top to bottom. That order follows the cues writing guides tell you to use, and it echoes Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking finding that readers scan headings and the start of lines when a page gives them cues (read at source).

Teach the disagreement honestly: that research is about web pages, not memos, and its own authors name several different scanning patterns, not one. No executive follows a fixed order every time. Read the bench for the ORDER of the versions and for what moves when you pull a lever; never quote its seconds as a forecast of your own boss. Structure for scanning in documentation is taught in Tech Writing, Structure readers can scan ↗; this bench is about the executive skim of a decision page.

Part 2 · covers what the four versions show

Length and order, not quality

6 min live

None of the four memos is badly written sentence by sentence. The draft is clear, the over-long version is thorough, the buried version has good headings. What separates them is where the decision, the ask and the risk sit, and how many words a reader has to pass to reach them.

LiveWhy the over-long memo loses the risk3 min▶

The over-long version starts well: "I recommend a shared customer record" is its first sentence. But it then spends a dozen sections on background, measurement, options, the build, the costs, lessons from earlier attempts and data protection, and its risks sit in the middle of a paragraph headed "Other considerations". The skimmer gets the decision early, the ask by 44.0 seconds, and the risk at 266.1 seconds, past four minutes.

That is the specific failure of thoroughness: the risk is there, and it is honest, and the reader who most needs it does not reach it. Session 5 gives the risk its own heading and its own first sentence; the rewrite does that, and reaches it in 24.7 seconds.

Self-studyTwo measures, because one can hide the other3 min▶

"Words before the ask" is measured: count the words a top-to-bottom reader passes before the first sentence that asks for something. "Read by 30 seconds" is modelled: it depends on the skim order and the multiplier. Keep both in view. The buried version reads 2 of 5 by 30 seconds, the same as the over-long one, but its reader passes 242 words before the ask against 1635. And the over-long memo's ask arrives earlier in the skim (44.0 seconds) than the buried one's (48.2 seconds), because its ask sits in the first sentence of its last paragraph, where a skimmer looks.

Real world

Short crisp paragraphs, detail to an appendix. On 9 August 1940 Churchill sent the War Cabinet a minute headed "Brevity" (The National Archives, CAB 67/8/11, catalogue read). As reported from a published transcription, it asked for main points in short, crisp paragraphs and for complicated factors or statistics to go to an appendix. The over-long memo is what that minute was written against; session 3 is how to move detail out without losing it.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever, the break button and the bench's limits

What makes it worse, and what the bench cannot tell you

6 min live

A bench that can only report good news measures nothing. This one has two ways to report a worse result: the anti-lever, which adds the background paragraph every first draft wants to open with, and the break button, which buries the ask.

LiveThe anti-lever, honestly sized2 min▶

Adding context up front always makes the ask later and always adds words before it: on every version, at every multiplier. Whether it also knocks an element past a checkpoint depends on how close the memo already sat. On the rewrite it costs the risk at 30 seconds; on the draft it costs the decision, the ask and the deadline at 90 seconds; on the buried version it changes no checkpoint at all, though the ask moves from 48.2 to 63.7 seconds. At 2.00x it changes no checkpoint on the rewrite either. The defensible claim is the first one: background on top never helps the skimming reader, and sometimes it costs them the thing they most needed.

LiveThe break button helps the draft, and that is the lesson3 min▶

Tick "Bury the ask" on the draft and the ask arrives sooner: 60.0 seconds instead of 75.5, with 308 words before it instead of 427. The draft had its ask in the very last paragraph, so moving it into paragraph 4 brings it forward. The break only hurts a memo that had its ask where a skimmer looks. That is the reason to run every lever on every version rather than trusting one result: a lever's effect depends on the memo it is pulled on.

Self-studyWhat the model leaves out4 min▶

The skim reader understands nothing. It counts an element as read when it finishes the sentence holding it, whether or not a real reader would grasp it at 357 words a minute. It never skips back, never stops early because the memo has bored it, and never reads Halvard's comment in the margin first. The keyword rules miss an ask phrased as "sign-off is needed" and would count "this is not a risk" as naming one; that is why tags override them.

The four versions are constructed, so the bench shows what ordering does to one set of facts, not how real executives behave. No real public decision memo with a clear reuse licence was added in this build; one published under an open licence would make a good fifth row. What survives these limits is the ordering lesson, and it agrees with every writing guide from Churchill's minute to Bottom Line Up Front: the reader gets what you put where they look.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Run your memo through the bench at 1.00x and 2.00x. If an element survives at one end and not the other, it is sitting too close to a checkpoint: move it up.
  2. Find your risk. When does the bench reach it? If it is past 30 seconds, give it its own heading and make it the first sentence under that heading.
  3. Pull both levers on your own memo. Write one sentence on what each one cost you, and one on why.
  4. Count your words before the ask on the last three memos you sent. Bring the largest to session 5.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The draft and the rewrite contain exactly the same facts. Why does the draft get 0 of 5 read by 30 seconds and the rewrite 5 of 5?

The draft is 449 words and the rewrite 402, so length is not the difference, and both run at the same rate. The bench has no bonuses; it reads in a fixed order, and only the order of the same facts changed.

2 · Which statement about the bench's numbers is accurate?

Nobody read these constructed memos. 238 is Brysbaert's measured average for careful silent reading; the 1.5 multiplier is an assumption. The model counts a sentence as read when it reaches the end, with no claim about understanding.

3 · "Bury the ask" makes the draft's ask arrive sooner, 60.0 seconds instead of 75.5. What is the right reading?

On the rewrite the same button takes the ask from 10.8 to 53.8 seconds and loses it at 30 seconds. A lever's effect depends on the memo it is pulled on, which is why every lever is run on every version.
