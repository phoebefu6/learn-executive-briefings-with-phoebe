Part 1 · covers the recommendation and the rejected options

Show the options you rejected, fairly

5 min live

The recommendation comes first, because the reader has already been told it on top. Then each option you did not choose gets one or two sentences, and the order inside those sentences matters: its real advantage first, then why it lost. A reader who sees you state the best case for the other options believes you when you say yours is better.

LiveAdvantage first, then why it lost3 min▶

Sunniva's draft dismissed the alternatives in a line each: "a one-off clean would not last" and "the CRM is not suitable". Both are conclusions with the reasoning removed, and Halvard, whose job is to check, will wonder what was removed with it. The rewrite gives each option its strongest true point first. The one-off clean is the cheapest option. Making the CRM the master suits sales, who already work in it every day.

Then, and only then, the reason it lost, stated as a consequence the reader can picture: the cleaned lists drift back within a year because nobody owns them; the depots end up entering data into a system built for account management. One or two sentences per option. If an option needs a paragraph to dismiss, it may not be the loser you think it is.

Self-studyThe straw man and the reader who knows3 min▶

An option written to lose, a straw man, is described in its weakest form so the recommendation looks inevitable. It works on a reader who knows nothing about the alternatives, and costs you badly with the one who does. At Fernhollow that reader is Rafe Okoro. The CRM account lists are his; he would read "the CRM is not suitable" as a judgment on his team, and he would be right that it misstates the case. If Marguerite asks Rafe what he thinks before she signs, and she may, he now has a reason to doubt everything else on the page.

The fair version costs Sunniva nothing. "Making the CRM the master suits sales" is true, Rafe agrees with it, and the reason it lost is about the depots, not about his team. The best test is to send the options paragraph to the person who would have chosen differently and ask whether you have stated their case as well as they would. Getting that conversation done before the memo lands is pre-wiring, taught in Stakeholder Management, Coalitions and pre-wiring ↗.

Presenting options is not choosing between them. How to compare options, weigh them and decide is taught in Decision Intelligence, session 6 ↗. Here the choice is already made, and the job is to show it to a reader who was not in the room when you made it.

Part 2 · covers naming the risk first

The risk you name before they do

5 min live

The main risk gets its own heading, and the risk itself is the first sentence under it. Below that, two more lines: what is already being done about it, and the signal that would stop the plan. A reader who finds the problem before you mention it starts wondering what else you left out. A reader who finds that you found it first relaxes. That is common practice in executive writing, not a measured law, and it holds for a plain reason: the writer who names the risk shows they looked.

LiveThree lines: risk, mitigation, stop signal3 min▶

The over-long version of Sunniva's memo had its risks, honestly stated, in the middle of a paragraph headed "Other considerations". On the session 4 bench the skimmer reached them at 266.1 seconds, past four minutes. The rewrite gives the main risk its own heading and makes it the first sentence under that heading, and the bench reaches it at 24.7 seconds. Same risk, same words, different place.

The block then does two more jobs. The mitigation says what is already happening, not what might: Rafe has agreed to name a joint owner before any build starts, so the CRM lists are opened up before the platform needs them. The stop signal tells Marguerite how she will know it is going wrong and what happens then: the rollout stops if the wrong-address credit-note rate does not fall. A risk with a stop signal is a risk someone is managing. Without one it reads as a worry.

Self-studyOne main risk, and where the second one goes3 min▶

Sunniva has two risks. The main one is the CRM lists launching the platform empty; the second is depot staff time during the cut-over. Only the main one gets the full block on the front page, because a list of seven equal risks tells the reader you have not decided which one matters. The second risk gets one line under the block, and any further ones go to the appendix, where session 3 put the detail that is there to be optional.

Choosing the main risk is a judgment, and it is worth testing on the person most exposed to it. For the depot risk that is Bram Tully, who is asked first on anything touching the depots. If he would rank it first, Sunniva should hear that before Marguerite does. Delivering a risk that is really bad news, the kind that changes the recommendation, is taught in Communication, Bad news and disagreement ↗.

Real world

Someone will look for it. Assume the person who checks the numbers will read your proposal for what could go wrong before reading why it will go right. At Fernhollow (constructed) that is Halvard, and Marguerite's reader card from session 1 already said she asks him before she signs. If Halvard finds the CRM risk himself, the conversation with Marguerite is about what Sunniva missed. If he finds it already named, with an owner and a stop signal, the conversation is about whether the stop signal is the right one.

Part 3 · covers confidence language

Say how sure you are, in words they will read your way

5 min live

"Likely", "probably", "a real chance": every reader turns these words into a number, and they do not all turn them into the same one. The fix is old and simple. Pick one fixed scale, use one word from it per key claim, and put the number in brackets the first time. Then say separately how good the evidence under that claim is, in its own sentence.

LiveKent's "serious possibility"3 min▶

In March 1951 a US National Intelligence Estimate, NIE 29-51, called an attack on Yugoslavia a serious possibility. Sherman Kent later told what happened next. He had meant odds of around 65 to 35 in favour of an attack. The Policy Planning Staff read it as much lower odds. When Kent asked members of the Board, their readings ran from about 20 to 80 to about 80 to 20. Everyone had signed the same phrase, and nobody had agreed on what it meant.

Kent's answer, in "Words of Estimative Probability" (CIA, Studies in Intelligence; text read at source, the 1964 date reported), was a chart pairing each phrase with odds: almost certain 93 (give or take about 6), probable 75 (about 12), chances about even 50 (about 10), probably not 30 (about 10), almost certainly not 7 (about 5). The lesson for a memo is not his exact numbers. It is that a hedge word with no scale behind it is read in whatever range the reader brings.

LiveOne scale, one sentence per key claim3 min▶

The US intelligence community now writes Kent's idea into its rules. ODNI's Intelligence Community Directive 203, Analytic Standards (2015, as amended; read at source) sets seven likelihood bands: almost no chance 01-05%, very unlikely 05-20%, unlikely 20-45%, roughly even chance 45-55%, likely 55-80%, very likely 80-95%, almost certain 95-99%. You do not have to adopt those bands, but you need one fixed scale, and borrowing a published one saves an argument about yours.

The rule for the memo: each key claim gets one confidence sentence, with a word from that scale and the number in brackets the first time it appears. After that the word alone will do, because the reader has been told what it means. Not every sentence needs one. The claims that carry the recommendation do: for Sunniva, that the shared record will cut wrong-address credit notes.

Self-studyHow likely, and how sure of the basis, are two sentences3 min▶

ICD 203 adds a rule that is easy to break without noticing: a product should not combine a confidence level and a degree of likelihood in the same sentence. "We are highly confident it is likely" mixes two different questions. How likely the outcome is belongs in one sentence. How good the evidence is, how much of it there is and how far it stretches, belongs in another. Kept apart, a reader can disagree with one without throwing out the other.

The evidence that numeric ranges barely cost a writer the reader's trust, while vague verbal hedges do, is laid out in Decision Intelligence, session 6 ↗, and the grades of uncertainty you can honestly promise are in Decision Intelligence, session 4 ↗. Here we only apply them: one scale, one sentence per key claim, the basis kept apart.

Real world

The phrase everyone signed. Kent's Board had put its name to one phrase in a national estimate. When he asked, its members' readings of it ran from about one in five to about four in five. If a room of professional estimators could do that with one phrase, a CFO and a COO reading "there is a good chance this pays back" on the same afternoon can do it too.

Build-along

Write the options, risk and confidence lines

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the memo you tested on the bench in session 4. If you have none, use Sunniva's. You will write the rejected options, the risk block and the confidence sentences, in that order, and finish with the full section stack under the top of the page. The worked lines below are the constructed Fernhollow Freight example; write yours beside them.

Write the rejected options. One or two sentences each. Start with the option's real advantage, the thing its strongest supporter would say, then why it lost, as a consequence the reader can picture. Then ask: would the person who preferred it sign off on how you described it?

★ Step 1 · options we did not chooseA one-off clean of the three lists is the cheapest option. It lost because, with no single owner, the lists drift back within a year. Making the CRM the master suits sales, who already work in it. It lost because the depots would end up entering data into a system built for account management.

Write the risk block. Its own heading. The risk is the first sentence. Then what is already being done, in the past or present tense, and the signal that would stop the plan. The second risk gets one line underneath.

★ Step 2 · main riskMain risk The CRM account lists may not be opened up early, so the platform would launch empty. Already done: Rafe has agreed to name a joint owner before any build starts. Stop signal: if the wrong-address credit-note rate does not fall at the first depot, the rollout stops. Second risk: depot staff time during the cut-over.

Write the confidence sentence for the key claim. One word from one fixed scale, the number in brackets the first time. Sunniva uses the ICD 203 band for "likely". The 55 to 80 percent is her judgment in the constructed case, never a measured fact, and the memo should not dress it up as one.

★ Step 3 · how likelyIt is likely (55 to 80 percent) that one shared record will cut wrong-address credit notes at the first depot. [Constructed Fernhollow Freight example: Sunniva's judgment, not a measurement.]

Write a separate sentence for how sure you are of the basis. Never in the same sentence as the likelihood. Say what the judgment rests on and how far that evidence stretches.

★ Step 4 · how good the basis isMy confidence in that judgment is moderate: it rests on one match of the three lists, run in September, which found they agree on name, address and contact for fewer than half of the 2,140 accounts. It does not yet show how fast the credit-note rate falls once the record is shared.

Now write your own. Use the same four slots for your memo. If you cannot name a stop signal, the plan may not have one yet, and that is worth knowing before the reader asks.

★ Step 5 · your memoOption not chosen: ____ Its real advantage: ____ It lost because: ____ Main risk: ____ Already done: ____ Stop signal: ____ It is ____ (__ to __ percent) that ____. My confidence in that judgment is ____: it rests on ____.

Rerun the bench. Paste the finished memo into the session 4 bench. The risk should now be reached early, because it is the first sentence under its own heading. If it is not, the heading is probably missing or the risk sits in the second sentence.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Test your options paragraph on the other side. Send it to one person who would have chosen a different option. Ask only: have I stated your option's case as well as you would?
  2. Find a stop signal. For your main risk, write the one observable thing that would make you stop or change course, and who would see it first.
  3. Audit your hedge words. In your last three sent documents, circle every "likely", "probably", "possibly" and "a good chance". How many came with a number or a scale?
  4. Pick your scale. Write down the one likelihood scale you will use from now on, ICD 203's or your own, and pin it where you write.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Sunniva needs to show why making the CRM the master lost. Which version belongs in the memo?

C states the option's real advantage first, then why it lost, as a consequence the reader can picture. A is a conclusion with the reasoning removed; B is a straw man by volume; D hides an option the reader may know about, which costs trust when they notice.

2 · Why does the main risk get its own heading and the first sentence under it?

Naming the risk first is common practice, and its reason is trust: you show you looked. The bench numbers are outputs of a stated model, not a study of executives, so B overclaims. The decision still goes on top (A), and one main risk with a stop signal beats a long list (C).

3 · What does ICD 203 say about confidence and likelihood, and what does Kent's "serious possibility" story show?

ICD 203 keeps the two questions in separate sentences, and its likelihood bands pair each word with a range. Kent meant about 65 to 35 in favour; Board members' readings ran from about 20 to 80 to 80 to 20, and the Policy Planning Staff read much lower odds. His fix was words with numbers attached, not a ban on words.
