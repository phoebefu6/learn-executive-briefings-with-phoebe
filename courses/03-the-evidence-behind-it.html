Learn Executive Briefings with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

The evidence behind it

The decision is on top and the format is chosen. Now the reader asks the question every executive asks next: why should I believe this? Tonight is about answering it in as few numbers as possible. Each claim gets one number and a source line the reader can check, the memo gets one exhibit at most, and everything else that is true and useful moves to an appendix, or out of the memo altogether.

Part 1 · covers one number per claim

One claim, one number, one source line

5 min live

Every claim in the body of a memo carries the one number that supports it and a source line: where the number came from, when, and how it was measured. One number gives the reader something to hold. The source line gives the person who checks, here Halvard, something to check. Three numbers for the same claim give the reader a choice they did not ask for.

LiveA source line answers where, when and how3 min▶

Sunniva's draft said "our customer data is badly out of sync", and then, over two paragraphs, gave a match rate from an earlier pilot, a count of duplicate records from the CRM report, and a percentage from a slide Rafe Okoro's team made last year. All three were true in their own way. None said how it was measured, and they did not agree with each other.

The rewrite keeps one: "fewer than half of 2,140 active accounts agree across the three lists (September match by my team, registration number or cleaned name and postcode)". The bracket is the source line. It says where (her team), when (September) and how (the matching rule). Halvard can ask for the method and get it. Marguerite can skip the bracket and still have the number.

The other two numbers are not wrong, and they are not lost: if one of them is the better measure, it replaces the first; if not, it goes to the appendix with the method. What it does not do is sit beside the first and make the reader choose.

Self-studyData, assumption, judgment: mark every sentence4 min▶

A memo mixes three kinds of sentence. Data: something measured, with a source line. Assumption: something taken as true to make the case work, such as a planner's own estimate of time lost or a build quote that has not been tendered. Judgment: what the writer concludes from the first two, such as "a one-off clean will drift back within a year".

All three belong in a decision memo. The problem is when the reader cannot tell them apart, because then a judgment borrows the weight of the data next to it. The fix is cheap: mark each claim in your working draft, and make the memo's wording carry the mark. "We measured", "we estimate", "we expect" and "I recommend" each tell the reader which kind of sentence they are reading.

How to say how likely a judgment is, in numbers or in words, is session 5. Why numeric ranges keep the reader's trust better than vague hedges is taught in Decision Intelligence, Uncertainty you can promise ↗; this session only asks you to say which sentences are which.

Self-studyWhy one number and not the best three2 min▶

Writers add numbers to look thorough. Each extra number for the same claim costs the reader something: they have to work out how the numbers relate, and the one that disagrees becomes the thing Halvard asks about in the meeting, instead of the decision. One number with a source line is a stronger claim than three without, because it can be checked.

Different claims can each have their number. The credit notes (about 310 last quarter for wrong-address deliveries) support a different claim from the match rate (what the mismatch costs, not how big it is). Five of six account managers keeping a private spreadsheet supports a third (people have stopped trusting the lists). Three claims, three numbers, three source lines.

Real world

Intelligence analysts are required to keep the three apart. The US intelligence community's analytic standards, Intelligence Community Directive 203 (signed 2 January 2015, as amended), list among the standards of analytic tradecraft that products distinguish the underlying information from analysts' assumptions and judgments: standard (3) (read at source). Analysts write for readers who decide under pressure and cannot check every source themselves. Executives are in the same position, which is why the discipline transfers.

Part 2 · covers the exhibit that earns its place

One exhibit, if it earns its place

5 min live

A one-page memo can carry one chart or one table, and only if it answers a question the reader will ask. Halvard will ask what it costs by year, so Sunniva's memo carries the cost table. The test for anything else is removal: take it out. If the reader loses no fact they need to decide, it was decoration.

LiveThe removal test3 min▶

Sunniva's draft had two charts. One showed how Fernhollow grew from four depots to eleven, which is why the three lists drifted apart. The other was the cost table. Run the test on each. Remove the growth chart: Marguerite still knows the decision, the cost, the deadline and the risk, and she lived through the growth herself. Remove the cost table: Halvard has a total with no split, and his first question in the meeting will be the one the table answered.

So the table stays and the chart goes. The test is not "is it interesting?" or "did it take work?" but "would the reader lose a decision-relevant fact?". Most second charts fail it, and many first ones do too. A memo with no exhibit is fine when the numbers fit in sentences.

Self-studyBuilding the one table3 min▶

Sunniva's table has two rows of cost and a total, split by year: platform and build €900,000 then €200,000; depot and sales time €180,000 then €120,000; €1.4 million over two years. All of it is constructed for teaching. Three choices make it work for the reader. The rows are the cost lines Halvard will recognise from a budget, not the project's work packages. The years are columns, because the question is "by year". The total is in the body text too, so Marguerite never has to read the table to know the cost.

The table also shows something a sentence would bury: depot and sales time is real money, €300,000 across the two years, and it is the cost most proposals leave out. Halvard checks whether the people cost is there; Odile Mercer checks whether her depots' time has been counted. One small table answers both.

Slides are a different job. An exhibit here is one chart or one table inside a written memo. Building charts for a deck, and slide design generally, is taught in Visual Storytelling ↗.

Part 3 · covers the appendix and what to cut

The appendix is where detail goes to be optional

5 min live

Evidence that is true and useful to someone, but not needed to decide, goes to the appendix. Evidence that does not change the decision goes nowhere. The body is what Marguerite reads; the appendix is what Halvard and Odile open when they want to check; the cut pile is what Sunniva keeps in her own files.

LiveWhat goes to the appendix3 min▶

The appendix test is "true, useful to someone named, and optional for the decider". Sunniva's matching method (registration number first, then cleaned name and postcode) is what Halvard will want if he doubts "fewer than half". The six account-manager interviews are what Rafe Okoro will want if he doubts that five of six keep a private spreadsheet. The per-depot rollout plan is what Odile and Bram Tully, the depot planning manager, will read line by line. Marguerite needs none of it to decide, and all of it needs to exist.

Point to the appendix from the body, once, at the claim it supports: "method in appendix A". That keeps the source line short and tells the checker where to go. An appendix nobody can find from the body is a second memo.

LiveWhat to cut entirely3 min▶

Three kinds of material leave the memo altogether. History that does not change the decision: how the depots grew is why the lists drifted, and Marguerite knows it. A second number for the same claim: Part 1 already chose one. And the unmeasured benefit dressed up as measured.

Sunniva believes one shared record will help Fernhollow keep its key accounts, because wrong deliveries annoy customers. That is plausible. Nobody has measured it. Her draft said "will improve retention on key accounts", which reads as a finding. The honest version names it and keeps it out of the case: "It may also help us keep key accounts; we have not measured that, and the case above does not depend on it." Halvard will find the unmeasured claim if it is dressed as measured, and once he finds one, he doubts the rest.

Self-studyWhy "everything in" loses the reader2 min▶

Session 4's bench has a version of Sunniva's memo with all of this left in: background, method, interviews, options, the rollout plan, lessons from earlier attempts. It is 1651 words, accurate, and opens with the recommendation. On the bench, at default settings, a skimming reader reaches its risk at 266.1 seconds, past four minutes. The seconds come from a stated model of a skimming reader, not a measurement, and session 4 shows how they move when you change its assumptions.

The lesson is not "shorter is always better". It is that every paragraph of true, optional detail in the body pushes the parts the decider needs further down the page. The appendix lets you keep the detail without paying that price.

Real world

Churchill sent statistics to the appendix. In a War Cabinet memorandum titled "Brevity", dated 9 August 1940 (The National Archives, CAB 67/8/11; catalogue read), Churchill is reported to have asked that reports set out their main points in "a series of short, crisp paragraphs", with complicated factors or statistics moved to an appendix (text reported from a published transcription). The format choices from that memo are in session 2; the appendix rule is the one that matters here.

Build-along

Sort the evidence for your memo

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Take the memo you started in session 1 and shaped in session 2. Each step shows Sunniva's version for the constructed Fernhollow Freight memo; write yours beside it. At the end you have a claim list, an exhibit decision, three bins, and one honest sentence.

List every claim in your draft. A claim is any sentence the reader has to believe for the recommendation to stand. Copy them out, one per line, in the order they appear. Then do the same for your own memo.

★ Step 1 · the claim list1. The three lists disagree on most accounts. 2. Wrong addresses cost us credit notes. 3. Account managers have stopped trusting the lists. 4. Planners lose time re-keying exceptions. 5. The proposal costs €1.4 million over two years. 6. A one-off clean will not last. 7. It will improve retention on key accounts.

Attach one number and a source line to each, and mark it. Where, when, how. Then the mark: data (measured), assumption (taken as true to make the case work) or judgment (your conclusion). If a claim has no number and no source, that is a finding too.

★ Step 2 · number, source line, mark1. fewer than half of 2,140 agree | Sept match, my team | data 2. about 310 credit notes, last qtr | billing records | data 3. five of six keep a private sheet | interviews, all six | data 4. about 20 min per planner per day | planners' own estimate | assumption 5. €1.4m over two years | quote + time estimate | assumption 6. drifts back within a year | our reading | judgment 7. (no number) | not measured | judgment

Choose the one exhibit, or none, with the removal test. For each chart or table in your draft, ask: if it were removed, would the reader lose a decision-relevant fact? Keep at most one.

★ Step 3 · the removal testCost table by year -> remove it: Halvard loses the split by year. KEEP. Depot growth chart -> remove it: nobody loses a fact they need to decide. CUT. Match-rate pie chart -> the sentence already carries "fewer than half". CUT.

Sort the rest into body, appendix and cut. Body: what the decider needs. Appendix: true, useful to a named checker, optional for the decider. Cut: does not change the decision. Name the reader for every appendix item.

★ Step 4 · three binsBODY claims 1, 2, 3, 5; cost table; main risk APPENDIX A matching method (for Halvard) B six AM interviews (for Rafe) C per-depot rollout plan (for Odile and Bram) CUT depot growth history; the pilot match rate; the CRM duplicate count

Rewrite the one sentence about the unmeasured benefit honestly. Name it, say it is not measured, and say the case does not rest on it. If your memo has no such sentence, look again: most do.

★ Step 5 · the honest sentenceBefore: The platform will improve retention on key accounts. After: It may also help us keep key accounts; we have not measured that, and the case above does not depend on it.

Claims 4 and 6 stay, with their marks showing. An assumption or a judgment is not a weakness in a decision memo; hiding which it is, is. "Planners estimate they lose about twenty minutes a day" is honest and still persuasive. Session 5 adds how confident you are in each judgment, in words the reader will read the same way you meant them.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Finish the claim list for your memo. Every claim with one number, a source line and a mark. Any claim with no source: find one, or reword it as an assumption or a judgment.
  2. Run the removal test on one document you received. Pick a report or deck from the last month and test each chart. Count how many would leave the reader without a fact they needed to decide.
  3. Write appendix A for your memo. The method behind your strongest number, written for the person most likely to check it. Add one pointer to it from the body.
  4. Bring the sorted memo, body only, as plain text. Session 4's bench reads it the way a skimming reader would and tells you when the decision, the ask, the deadline, the cost and the risk arrive.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Sunniva writes "fewer than half of 2,140 active accounts agree across the three lists". What must the source line after it say?

A source line lets the checker check: where, when, how. A says who but not how; B is a claim about the number rather than a source for it; C adds competing numbers the reader then has to reconcile. The full method goes to the appendix, with one pointer from the body.

2 · Sunniva's memo already carries the cost table. She also has a good chart of how Fernhollow grew from four depots to eleven. Should it go in the body?

The removal test asks whether the reader would lose a decision-relevant fact. Marguerite knows the company grew; the chart changes nothing she decides. The cost table stays because Halvard will ask what it costs by year. D is too strong: one exhibit is fine when it earns its place.

3 · Sunniva believes the shared record will help keep key accounts, but nobody has measured it. What should the memo do with that?

An unmeasured benefit dressed as measured is the claim Halvard finds first, and it costs the rest of the memo his trust. C names it honestly and makes clear the case does not rest on it. B loses a real consideration; D invents a number.
